MAIN PROJECTS ABOUT

Introduction

The game was originally inspired by Cellular Automata (CA), a system pioneered by John von Neumann and Stanislaw Ulam.
It is truly fascinating how such a system can generate dynamic phenomena, and I saw an opportunity to translate that dynamic behaviour into dynamic decision-making for players. Sid Meier, the creator of Civilization, famously stated that "a game is a series of interesting decisions," which aligns perfectly with algorithms driven by dynamic systems.
A key limitation of conventional simulation games is that some players find them too complicated because they must learn every game feature, even though the core appeal stems from the freedom of controlling a game system. That is why I started this project: to create an easy-to-understand game centered on dynamic, open-ended decision-making.

Process

I began the project with MICROVERSE, where I gained fundamental knowledge of low-level physics, optimization, and emergent systems.
However, playtesting revealed that players did not find it engaging because executing decisions had little impact on the game's outcome. According to Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) theory, feedback is essential for player motivation. Because players in MICROVERSE acted as just one of thousands of automated agents, there was little room for meaningful choice. At the same time, I could not grant the player significantly greater influence than the automated agents without directly contradicting the core philosophy of emergence. Furthermore, in a conventional particle life system, agents can only detect nearby entities due to performance constraints, which further limited the player's reach.
To resolve this design challenge, I integrated an ant colony algorithm on top of the existing particle life system. Each agent deposits "pheromones" onto a grid-based field, allowing wandering agents to detect these trails and alter their trajectory accordingly. This mechanism enables agents to perceive and react to entities across a much wider area than conventional particle life systems allow.

Distinctive Control

To manage thousands of agents efficiently, I designed a distinctive system where players spray pheromones directly onto the field to guide agent behaviour. This approach makes thousands of agents highly responsive, enhancing player motivation through clear feedback loops grounded in Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and HCI principles, while preserving optimization via array-based grid calculations.

Behavioural Studies and Game Design Intention

Applying conventional behavioural psychology to game design cannot fully explain why a game is compelling, as games are multi-layered media. Furthermore, over-relying on psychological frameworks before establishing a stable system can constrain creativity; in practice, psychological analysis is most effective during and after development. Nevertheless, analysing behavioural psychology remains a valuable practice because player behaviour can be influenced and measured.

Skinner's Operant Conditioning

According to Skinner's theory of operant conditioning, behaviour is reinforced through rewards (positive reinforcement) and the removal of negative stimuli (negative reinforcement). In this game, players are motivated when their agents reproduce after consuming food (positive reinforcement), or when rival enemy agents starve to death (negative reinforcement). Although no explicit UI pops up when a reward is triggered, these reinforcement structures are directly embedded into the core system mechanics. Tetris is a great example of this principle: players remain engaged not because of flashy UI effects, but because of the fundamental reward-and-punishment structure inherent in stacking blocks.

Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment (DDA) and Flow State

Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment (DDA) is systematically integrated into the game loop. When blue agents dominate the field (indicating high player skill), red enemy agents have more opportunities to consume blue agents and reproduce. Conversely, if blue agents are scarce (indicating player struggle or heavy enemy pressure), fewer red enemies appear because they fail to feed in time and starve. This mechanism maintains consistent tension regardless of skill level, dynamically altering game difficulty to keep players in a state of flow, where player skill and game challenge remain balanced.

Self-Determination Theory (SDT): Competence, Autonomy, and Relatedness

I targeted all three core motivators outlined in Self-Determination Theory (SDT): Competence, Autonomy, and Relatedness.

Competence: Players stay engaged when they feel their skill is improving. To support this, I intentionally designed the system to be as responsive as possible to player input, ensuring failure feels like a direct result of player execution rather than system friction. Furthermore, players must continually adapt their tactics to survive longer.

Autonomy: Players experience autonomy when they feel they can control the system without artificial restrictions. By using drawing gestures, players can dynamically guide blue agents however they choose, offering agency that feels flexible rather than rigid or scripted with high-dimensional decision-making.

Relatedness: Relatedness involves feeling socially connected or valued within a group. While often overlooked in single-player experiences, I included a post-game score panel to foster connection. During showcase and festival playtests, this feature performed surprisingly well: players frequently snapped photos of their high scores to compare and share achievements with others.

Outcome Uncertainty, High Stakes, and the Near-Miss Effect

When only a single blue agent remains, its survival value spikes dramatically; a single misstep can trigger a game over. The uncertainty of whether that final agent will reach food creates high tension. If the attempt fails, the near-miss effect triggers positive frustration (unlike negative frustration caused by poor system design such as latency or bugs), which encourages the player to immediately try again.
